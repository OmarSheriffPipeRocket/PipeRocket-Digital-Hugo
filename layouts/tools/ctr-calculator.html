What is CTR (click-through rate)?

Click-through rate is the share of people who saw an ad, listing, or link and clicked it. It is the standard signal for whether a headline, offer, or ad position is actually earning attention, and it feeds directly into cost per click and Quality Score on paid platforms.

How this calculator works

The formula:

CTR                    = Clicks ÷ Impressions × 100
Clicks needed at target = Impressions × Target CTR
Extra clicks needed     = Clicks needed at target − Current clicks

CTR only means something next to a benchmark for the same channel. A 1.5% CTR is weak for search ads and excellent for display ads, so always compare within the same ad type.

What is a good CTR?

Across the 65+ B2B SaaS Google Ads accounts PipeRocket manages, the average CTR is 3.60%, and brand search CTR (22.2%) runs roughly 6x higher than non-brand search CTR (3.6%), since searcher intent drives the gap more than ad creative. See the full Google Ads benchmarks report.

How to improve CTR

Match Your Headline to the Exact Search Term

Quality Score rewards relevance, and relevance is judged largely by how closely your headline mirrors what was typed into the search box. A searcher scanning results decides in under a second whether an ad actually answers their exact query.

A generic headline built to cover ten keywords will underperform ten narrow ad groups each built around one keyword. Split broad ad groups into tightly themed ones, sometimes called single-keyword ad groups, keeping each to a handful of closely related terms.

Write the headline as if answering that one search directly, not a whole category of searches. This is the single highest-leverage CTR fix on paid search, it costs nothing beyond restructuring the account, and the lift compounds into a lower cost per click.

Lead With a Number, Not a Vague Claim

A number reads as evidence; an adjective reads as a claim, because the reader can judge a number instantly and has to take an adjective on faith. A few swaps that hold up across most B2B SaaS accounts:

  • "Affordable pricing" → "$84 average cost per lead"
  • "Trusted by many teams" → "Used by 65+ SaaS teams"
  • "Fast results" → "Live in 14 days"
  • "Great support" → "24-hour response time"

Put the number as close to the front as character limits allow, since Responsive Search Ads cap each headline at 30 characters and mobile truncation often cuts the back half of the line. Run at least two variants per ad group and let three to four weeks of data pick the winner.

Use Every Ad Extension Your Format Supports

Sitelinks, callouts, structured snippets, and price extensions add extra lines and links underneath the core ad, taking up more space on the results page and giving the searcher more surface area to click. Accounts that skip extensions leave free CTR gains on the table, since Google does not charge extra for the added real estate.

On the organic side, the equivalent move is schema markup. FAQ, review, and product schema can add star ratings, price, or expandable questions directly in the search snippet, pulling the eye the same way an ad extension does. Both are one-time setup work that keeps paying off on every future impression.

Fix the Audience Before You Touch the Copy

A perfectly written headline shown to the wrong audience will always underperform a mediocre headline shown to the right one, because CTR is a function of relevance to the viewer, not just quality of the writing. Before rewriting ad copy, work through this list:

  • Negative keywords are current and actually blocking the obviously poor-fit searches
  • Audience layering (in-market, affinity, customer match) isn't diluting spend on poor-fit searchers
  • Geographic and device targeting match where your actual buyers are, not just where the budget was set to run
  • Device-level performance isn't hiding a mobile-truncation problem behind a healthy blended average

Most CTR problems that look like a "copy problem" are actually a targeting problem wearing a copy problem's clothes, and no amount of headline testing fixes wrong-audience impressions.

Rewrite Title Tags and Meta Descriptions Like Ad Copy

The same click-earning logic that governs paid ads governs organic listings: the title tag and meta description are your ad copy for a result you did not pay for.

Front-load the primary keyword and the core benefit in the title tag, since Google truncates past roughly 60 characters, and write the meta description as a direct answer to the query rather than a vague summary.

Pull the exact-match query CTR for that page from Search Console first, so you rewrite the pages with the biggest visible gap. Pages that already rank on page one but sit at a low CTR are usually losing on snippet appeal, not relevance, a much faster fix than earning new rankings.

Frequently asked questions

How do you calculate CTR?

CTR = clicks divided by impressions, times 100. 750 clicks on 50,000 impressions gives a CTR of 1.5%. Use the same time period and the same source for both numbers.

What is a good CTR?

It depends heavily on channel. Across 65+ B2B SaaS Google Ads accounts PipeRocket manages, average CTR is 3.6%, with brand search running around 22.2% and display ads 0.3 to 0.6%. Compare to your own channel and past performance, not one universal number.

Why is my CTR lower than the benchmark?

Usually weak ad copy or headline, a mismatch between the keyword and the offer, wrong audience targeting, or a low ad position. On search ads, position and extensions move CTR independently of the copy.

How can I improve my CTR?

Tighten keyword-to-ad match, lead with the benefit or a number in the headline, add ad extensions or rich snippets, and test multiple headlines. For organic listings, rewrite title tags and meta descriptions.
