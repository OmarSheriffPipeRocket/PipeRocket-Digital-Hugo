What is an SEO budget?

An SEO budget is the amount a company sets aside each month for SEO work: strategy, technical fixes, content, and link building. That spend can go to an agency retainer or an in-house team plus tools.

Unlike a PPC budget, there is no bid you can dial up or down in real time, so the number has to be planned around the scope of work rather than a live auction.

SEO also has no single universal formula the way CAC or CTR do, because the "right" number depends on your keyword mix, how content gets produced, and who does the work. This calculator estimates a range from those inputs, rather than pretending a single number is exact.

How this calculator works

The formula:

Content cost   = Keywords × Cost per page (traditional writing only, editable)
Strategy cost  = Hours × Rate, OR a flat retainer, OR a flat salary
Link building  = Link building cost (pre-filled from mix, editable)
Subtotal       = Content cost + Strategy cost + Link building + Tools
Monthly budget = Subtotal × Site-authority multiplier
Budget range   = Monthly budget ± 15%

Content and strategy are priced separately because they behave differently. Content cost scales directly with page count when you pay a human writer per page, while strategy, technical guidance and reporting scale more with ongoing management than raw output.

Choosing AI-assisted content removes the content-cost line entirely, since the marginal cost of a page is closer to your existing tool subscription and team time than a per-page vendor fee. Cost per page and link building cost both pre-fill from this calculator's benchmark, but stay editable.

Competition mix replaces a single tier, since blending percentages across low, medium and high keywords tracks a real list far better than forcing every keyword into one bucket. The ±15% range still applies for the same reason as before: matching inputs can still hide a heavier technical cleanup on one side.

What's a realistic SEO budget range?

Search Engine Journal's benchmark survey puts most SEO budgets between $500 and $10,000 a month, with the single most common band being $1,001 to $5,000. Semrush puts the average US SEO agency hourly rate at $147.93, which is why this calculator defaults to $150.

These ranges track published freelance and agency pricing: a standard 1,500-word post commonly runs $250 to $399, and senior B2B content built to compete for harder terms runs toward $800 to $1,500. There is no equivalent per-page table for AI-assisted content, since that path does not carry a separate content-cost line in this calculator.

The mix option blends per-page prices using assumed tier splits: majority low is 70/20/10 across low/medium/high, majority medium is 20/60/20, majority high is 10/20/70, and mixed is roughly a third each.

Site authority then adjusts the whole total: roughly +25% for a new site under a year old, no change for a growing site with some rankings, about -8% for a mid-authority site with steady rankings, and -15% for an established site with deep authority already built.

Link building cost pre-fills from a baseline scaled by the same blended tier multiplier as content, since a harder keyword mix usually needs a heavier link campaign. Type in a real vendor quote and the total recalculates off that instead.

How to set your SEO budget

Start From the Scope of Work, Not a Round Number

Many businesses set an SEO budget by picking a figure that sounds reasonable, something like $1,000 or $2,500 a month, rather than working backward from what the work actually requires. That approach usually either overpays for a small keyword set or underfunds a genuinely competitive one.

Start instead by listing your actual target keywords and roughly grouping them by competition. A handful of long-tail terms with weak page-one results need far less content investment than a dozen head terms where established competitors already rank.

Once you have that list, the model above gives you a defensible starting number to bring into a vendor conversation, instead of anchoring on whatever number you saw in a blog post.

It also gives you a number you can defend internally when finance asks why the line item is what it is, since it traces back to a keyword list and a mix, not a gut feeling.

Estimate Your Keyword Mix Honestly, Not Just One Tier

This calculator does not assume every keyword sits at one tier, since real keyword lists rarely do. Choose the mix option that best reflects your actual list: mostly low, mostly medium, mostly high, or a genuine spread across all three.

  • Are the top 10 results dominated by large, well-known brands with strong domain authority
  • Do page-one competitors already have deep, well-structured content on the exact topic
  • Does the keyword carry clear commercial intent that draws paid competition too
  • Has nobody bothered to build a dedicated page for it yet, which usually signals a lower tier

Run each keyword through these checks and count roughly how many land in each tier. If most trip two or more of the first three, treat your list as majority high even if the raw search volume looks modest, since volume signals demand, not effort.

Plan for a 6 to 12 Month Runway, Not a Single Month

SEO compounds, which means the number that matters is not one month's spend but the total committed over a realistic runway. A budget that looks generous for month one can look thin if it is not sustained past month three, since rankings built on published content keep taking months to mature.

Multiply this calculator's monthly output by at least six, and ideally twelve, before deciding whether a budget is actually affordable. A business that can fund $5,000 a month for two months and then stop has effectively budgeted less than one that funds $3,000 a month for a full year.

If the twelve-month total does not fit comfortably in your marketing plan, scale down the keyword count or mix rather than the runway.

Match the Engagement Model to How You Want to Manage Risk

This calculator's engagement input reflects three real ways SEO work gets priced: an agency billing hourly, an agency billing a flat monthly retainer, or an in-house hire on salary. Each changes what counts as a fixed cost versus a variable one.

Hourly billing scales cleanly with scope: a bigger keyword list means more billed hours, which pairs naturally with this calculator's mix model. A retainer bundles that variability into one flat number instead, simpler to budget but able to over- or under-deliver hours some months.

An in-house hire trades either model for a fixed salary that does not flex with scope, and rarely covers every discipline alone. Most in-house SEO teams still contract out specialist link building or a periodic technical audit, so the true cost is salary plus tools plus a smaller outside budget.

Don't Mistake "No Content Line" for "No Content Cost"

Switching to AI-assisted production removes the per-page cost line from this calculator, because the marginal cost of generating a draft is closer to a tool subscription than a vendor invoice. That does not mean the work is free, it means the cost moved somewhere else in the model.

It shows up as more strategy hours instead: reviewing an AI draft for accuracy, structuring it around real search intent, and editing it into your brand voice all take real time, and that time belongs in the hourly, retainer or salary line, not a separate content fee.

  • Fact-checking claims and statistics before publishing
  • Structuring content around real search intent, not a generic outline
  • Editing for brand voice and E-E-A-T signals
  • Adding first-hand experience or proprietary data AI cannot generate

If your strategy hours or retainer scope does not already assume that review time, an AI-assisted budget that looks cheaper on paper can quietly produce thinner content than a traditional one, not a better deal.

Weight the Budget for Your Site's Existing Authority

Two sites targeting the identical keyword list can need very different budgets depending on where they start. A brand-new domain with no backlink profile and thin existing content needs meaningfully more link building and content investment just to earn the authority competitors already have, before any ranking gains even start to show up.

An established site with years of published content and a real backlink profile can often win the same keywords with less incremental work, building on existing authority rather than creating it from zero. A mid-authority site, one with steady rankings and a moderate domain rating but not yet a category leader, sits between the two.

That middle case is common enough that this calculator now offers four authority levels instead of three, since treating a two-year-old site with a handful of ranking pages the same as a brand-new domain or a market leader would badly distort the estimate either way.

Treat a Rate Far Below the Benchmark as a Red Flag

A quote that comes in at half your calculated range is not automatically a bargain. It usually means the vendor is planning far fewer hours than your keyword mix actually requires, or filling those hours with templated content and automated link placements instead of real work.

  • No clear breakdown of hours or deliverables behind the price
  • Guaranteed rankings or a fixed timeline for organic results
  • Link building described only as "outreach" with no sample targets
  • Reporting limited to rank-tracker screenshots with no narrative

Any one of these on its own is worth a follow-up question; two or more together are a reason to compare the quote against this calculator's estimate before signing. A rate that undercuts the benchmark by half is buying fewer hours, not a better deal.

Frequently asked questions

How do you calculate an SEO budget?

Multiply keywords by a blended dollar-per-page rate for your mix and content method, add a strategy cost (hourly, retainer, or salary), add link building and tools, then apply a site-authority multiplier. 20 majority-medium keywords with traditional writing and hourly strategy comes to roughly $17,800 for a growing site, a $15.2K to $20.5K range.

What's a typical monthly SEO budget?

Industry surveys put most SEO budgets between $500 and $10,000 a month, with the single most common band being $1,001 to $5,000, according to Search Engine Journal's benchmark data. Where a business lands in that range depends mostly on keyword count and competition mix, not company size alone.

What does an SEO budget usually cover?

A full SEO scope covers strategy and keyword research, technical fixes, content creation, and link building or digital PR, plus tools and software. That work gets priced through an agency hourly rate, an agency retainer, or an in-house salary, and content itself can be traditionally written or AI-assisted.

Is a cheap SEO retainer worth it?

Rarely. A quote well below the blended-rate benchmark for the stated scope usually means fewer hours than the work requires, template content, or automated link building that risks a penalty. Compare any quote against this calculator's estimate for your actual keyword mix, not just the sticker price.
